The link between IB_memo_v6 and accumulate-to-bound decision-making
I read IB_memo_v6.md in full and pulled the Fan et al. 2018 eLife paper to check how you're currently talking about session-to-session variability. That's enough to sketch a real plan rather than a generic one. Here's the shape of it.

The key translation the memo doesn't make yet
IB_memo_v6 is built entirely on the bead task — a volatile, non-terminating tracking problem. The normative variable L_t never stops; it's stabilized by the hazard rate h, and the optimal capacity-limited machine (§6–7) is a non-absorbing bounded ladder: a leaky accumulator that holds a category forever, updating as evidence arrives.

Your DDM work is the opposite regime: a stationary generative process (fixed drift per trial), and the accumulator is absorbing — it terminates at a bound and that termination is the decision, with RT as the readout. In the memo's own terms, this is the h → 0 limit: no discounting, ψ(L) = L, so L_t is unbounded LLR accumulation — which is exactly the log-likelihood-ratio form of the SPRT that Gold & Shadlen (2002, 2007) already established as the normative account of your tasks, and which the DDM implements as its continuum limit.

So the first real theoretical move isn't "apply the memo" — it's extend it: replace the non-absorbing stabilizing bound with an absorbing decision bound, and ask what the IB-optimal discrete accumulator looks like when capacity is limited and the process must terminate. That's a genuinely new derivation, but it inherits almost everything from §6–7 of the memo (state-diagram machinery, the deterministic-machine search, the convex-hull test for abrupt vs. continuous transitions) — you're swapping the boundary condition, not rebuilding the tool.

1. Theoretical link: DDM parameters as a signature of capacity
Once you make that swap, the object you get is a quantized random walk with absorbing barriers — which is not a new invention; it's the classical discrete-time random-walk model that Ratcliff & Smith (2004) already show converges to the DDM as step size → 0 and step rate → ∞. The memo's contribution is that it tells you the walk isn't a coarsened approximation of the continuous process — it's what an information-optimal encoder produces under a capacity constraint, with a specific, derivable structure (§2.1–2.2): few states, unevenly spaced, concentrated where (occupancy) × (usefulness) peaks, sharpened before split.

That gives you a principled way to ask what a capacity cut does to standard DDM fits:

Bound height a (in units of diffusion noise) is the natural proxy for capacity, because the number of resolvable levels between start and bound is bounded by 2^{I(X;R)}. A low-capacity observer can't be modeled as "the same DDM with smaller a" — it's a DDM whose accumulator takes discrete, unevenly-spaced jumps, so the mapping to a is not identity. This needs to be derived (moment-matching or exact first-passage-time calculation on the discrete chain), but the qualitative prediction is: capacity changes masquerade as changes in a, but leave a distinguishable residual in RT-distribution shape that pure bound changes don't produce.
Drift v/k should be comparatively protected. In the memo, SNR sets the ceiling and β_c, but capacity sets the number of categories (§3, Fig. 4g–h) — these are separable knobs, with state-count onsets moving <0.06 bits across a 2.5× range of d'. Translated: if session-to-session variability is capacity-driven, it should show up disproportionately in bound/collapsing-bound/urgency-like parameters, much less in the drift/sensitivity parameter k that Fan et al. already track separately from bound height a.
Non-decision time and starting point z are the natural place to look for the "sharpen, then split" structure (§2.1c): the criterion location is fixed early, and what changes with capacity is the steepness of the criterion, not its position — this predicts collapsing-bound- or urgency-like signatures (progressive sharpening) that aren't driven by a deadline at all, but by capacity.
The concrete deliverable here is a derivation, not a metaphor: extend ib_core.py's recursive-machine search to absorbing boundaries, compute exact first-passage RT/accuracy for the k-state IB-optimal chain, and fit a standard DDM to synthetic data generated from it. Whatever parameter absorbs the capacity manipulation in that fit is your answer to "what does a capacity change look like in DDM terms," and it's checkable against real data instead of asserted.

2. Behavioral signatures, and how to fit this with limited trials
Two predictions fall out of the memo directly, without new derivation:

Confidence/discreteness (§2.4). The memo's sharpest, most literature-distinguishing prediction is that a capacity-limited observer holds a binary or few-category belief with a sharp criterion, not a graded one — "what varies with capacity is how sharp the criterion is, not how graded the belief is." If you have confidence-report data from any of these tasks, this is directly testable: graded-belief accounts (including standard DDM confidence-as-balance-of-evidence) predict smooth covariation of confidence with accumulated evidence/RT; the IB account predicts a step function, with capacity changing the steepness of the step, not its shape.
Reframing "inefficiency" (§6). The memo shows that a perfectly optimal recursive observer, scored against the one-shot bound, looks 62–87% "efficient" in the low-capacity range purely from the recursion constraint — not from suboptimality. This is a warning and an opportunity: any variability you've been attributing to noise/suboptimality in fitted efficiency measures should first be checked against what a capacity-constrained-but-optimal recursive observer would produce. This is exactly the reinterpretation Fan et al. 2018 gestures at when they attribute systematic deviations from reward-maximizing bounds to "satisficing and heuristic principles" rather than poor learning — the IB account offers a third option: neither miscalibration nor heuristic satisficing, but rational allocation of a shared, limited representational budget. Worth checking whether the direction and pattern of their reward-asymmetry deviations lines up with what a capacity-limited bound-setting process would predict, versus their gradient-following satisficing account.
On fitting individual/session data with few hundred trials — the practical constraint you flagged — the move is to not estimate the IB frontier from trial data at all. The frontier is a property of the task's generative statistics (hazard, SNR, or drift/noise for stationary tasks), which you already estimate robustly per session via standard DDM fitting. So:

Fit the ordinary generative parameters per session (drift/noise, or hazard, as you already do) — this is the part that needs the trial count, and you already have it working.
Compute the IB frontier for that session's task statistics offline, exactly as the memo does (§8) — this costs no trials at all, it's a numerical solve.
Fit only where on that frontier the session sits — a 1–2 parameter fit (capacity I(X;R), or equivalently β) — using a likelihood built from the discretized accumulator's predicted choice/RT distribution rather than a continuous-DDM likelihood.
That's a much lower-dimensional fitting problem than a full DDM refit, which is what makes it tractable at session-level trial counts. Hierarchical partial-pooling across sessions/subjects (which this lab already does for DDM parameters) would stabilize it further.

3. Neural predictions
Three that are directly checkable against data you already have:

Discreteness in ramping activity. If the represented decision variable is quantized into k states rather than continuous, LIP/FEF ramping activity (Law & Gold; Ding & Gold FEF/caudate work) should show clustering around a small number of firing-rate "rungs" at fixed time bins — beyond what Poisson spiking variability alone predicts — with the number of rungs varying by session/subject in a way that should correlate with the behaviorally-fit capacity parameter from #2.
Discrete attractor structure over continuous line-attractor structure. Population-level analyses (energy-landscape reconstruction, or simpler: multimodality of population state at fixed time-in-trial) should show a small number of discrete stable states in low-capacity sessions, rather than the continuous manifold assumed by standard line-attractor/Wong-Wang-style accounts.
Arousal/state as a capacity knob. This lab already has the Aston-Jones & Gold (2009) and Nassar/Gold pupil-linked work on exactly this bead/change-point paradigm. A very direct prediction: pupil-indexed arousal state should predict the fitted capacity parameter on a session or even epoch basis, with low-arousal periods showing more quantized (step-like) behavior — this connects an existing physiological measure directly to the memo's central construct without needing new recordings.
The basal ganglia angle (Ding & Gold caudate work on flexible decision rules) is also worth flagging as a mechanism candidate for where discretization is imposed — cortico-basal ganglia gating as the implementation of "collapse continuous cortical evidence into a categorical commit state" — but that's a second-order hypothesis, worth having in mind rather than pursuing first.

Proposed plan
Derive the absorbing-bound extension. Extend ib_core.py's recursive-machine search (§6–7 machinery) to terminating/absorbing states; get exact FPT-based RT and accuracy for the IB-optimal k-state chain in the stationary (h=0) case. This is the load-bearing piece everything else depends on.
Characterize the DDM-parameter signature. Fit standard DDM to synthetic data generated from step (1)'s process across a range of capacities; identify which DDM parameters absorb the capacity manipulation and what residual signature (RT-shape, confidence-step, history-nonlinearity) distinguishes it from a genuine bound/drift change. This answers Q1 concretely instead of by analogy.
Build the session-level capacity estimator described in Q2 (frontier computed from fitted generative parameters, then a 1–2 parameter fit for where the session sits on it), and validate it on synthetic data before touching real sessions.
Run it on existing behavioral data — Fan et al.'s reward-bias sessions are the natural first target since you already have per-session DDM parameter estimates and a documented variability puzzle to re-examine; the Glaze-paradigm datasets underlying the memo itself are the second, since the memo's numbers already describe where subjects sit on the filtering frontier.
Check confidence data if it exists for the categorical-criterion prediction (§2.4) — this is the single sharpest behavioral discriminator between this account and standard graded-belief models, worth prioritizing if any dataset has confidence reports.
Neural pass, once a behavioral capacity estimate exists per session: test ramping-activity discreteness and pupil-capacity correlation in whichever LIP/FEF/caudate dataset has the best session count and, ideally, pupil recorded alongside.
